Research Management

Management and research are fundamentally distinct activities. Management is about optimizing available resources to achieve specific outcomes, often relying on past experiences to guide decision-making. It involves careful planning, strategic allocation of resources, and logical thinking to maximize efficiency.

Research, on the other hand, is the pursuit of solutions, truth, or information—often without a clear direction. From a management perspective, this trial-and-error approach may seem like the dumbest thing, as it lacks the efficiency and predictability that management strives for. However, research thrives on repeated experimentation, where seemingly pointless or ‘dumb’ efforts eventually lead to groundbreaking discoveries. Many of history’s greatest innovations were accidental byproducts of persistence and curiosity rather than careful planning. This paradox is humorously illustrated in a meme where a team struggling with a load on square wheels is too busy to test a round one—highlighting how the immediate focus on efficiency can sometimes overlook revolutionary improvements.

The career trajectory of a researcher in a typical Indian research center follows a structured progression. A researcher begins as the junior-most member, focusing entirely on research (100% research, 0% management). As their career advances, the research-to-management ratio gradually shifts. Initially, they take on responsibilities such as managing academic projects. Later, they oversee junior researchers and progressively move up the organizational hierarchy, with management responsibilities increasing over time.

At the peak of their career, a researcher in an organization often transitions to 0% research and 100% management. The speed at which a researcher adapts to this shift from research to management largely determines their rate of advancement within the organizational hierarchy. This could explain why our research centers are producing more great managers than great scientists.

Inspired by this observation, I propose a bold restructuring of research organizations one where every scientist is simply a 'Researcher,' free from hierarchical titles that dilute their focus. Their merit would be measured purely by objective research indices like citation scores, h-index, patent values, and authorships, ensuring that recognition is based on innovation rather than administrative rank. Meanwhile, a separate management layer completely independent of research would handle all administrative and logistical tasks, allowing scientists to do what they do best: push the boundaries of knowledge without managerial distractions.

There must be a reason why such a system doesn’t already exist—perhaps practical challenges or the need for balance between research and management. Still, it’s an interesting idea worth exploring.
